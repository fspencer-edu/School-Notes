## Aggregate Demand in Keynesian Analysis

- Keynesian perspective focuses on aggregate demand
- Firms produce output only if they expect it to sell
- Recessionary gap
- Inflationary gap

### What Determines Consumption Expenditure?

- Consumption expenditure
	- Spending by households and individuals on durable goods, nondurable goods, and services
- Durable goods
	- Items that last and provide value over time
- Non durable goods
	- Groceries
- Services
	- Intangible goods that consumers buy

3 Factors that affect consumption
1. Disposable income
2. Expected future income
3. Wealth or credit

### What Determines Investment Expenditure?

- 4 categories of investments
	- Producer's durable equipment and software
	- Non-residential structures
	- Inventories
	- Residential structures

- Expectations of future profits
- Interest rate

### What Determined Government Spending?

- Government spending
	- Federal
	- State
	- Local
- Tax rates

### What Determines Net Exports?

- Export expenditures add to AD, while import subtracts from AD

- Changes in relative growth rates between countries
- Changes in relative prices between countries

## The Building Blocks of Keynesian Analysis

- Explains why recessions and depressions occur
- Offers a policy to minimize their effects
	- Aggregate demand is not always automatically high enough to provide firms with an incentive to hire to full employment
	- Macroeconomy may adjust only slowly to shifts in aggregate demand
		- Sticky wages and prices
			- Do not respond to decreases or increases in demand

- Recessions occurs when the level of demand for goods and services is less than what is produced when labour is fully employed

### Wage and Price Stickiness

- Coordination argument
	- A market-oriented economy has no obvious way of coordinated wage reductions
	- Businesses avoid wage cuts because they may depress morale and hurt productivity
- Sticky prices
	- Changing prices uses company resources
	- Menu costs

### The Two Keynesian Assumptions in the AD/AS Model

- Stickiness of wages and prices, cause the aggregate supply curve to be flatter than than the supply curve (labour or specific good)
- Macroeconomic externality
	- What happens at the macro level is different from what happens at the micro level

### The Expenditure Multiplier

- Spending affects the equilibrium level of GDP
- A change in spending causes a more than proportionate change in the GDP

$\frac{\Delta Y}{\Delta Spending} > 1$

- One person's spending becomes another person's income
- Leads to additional spending and additional income
- Cumulative impact on GDP is larger than the initial increase in spending
- Multiplier operates in a negative and positive direction
	- When investment spending collapses, is causes a larger decrease in real GDP

### The Discovery of the Philip Curve

- Phillips curve
	- Tradeoff between the unemployment rate and the inflation rate

<img src="/images/Pasted image 20260512145332.png" alt="image" width="500">

### The Instability of the Philip Curve

- Low inflation and high unemployment
- High inflation and low unemployment

- Economies use fiscal and monetary policies to move up or down the curve
- Stagflation
	- Increase in both unemployment and inflation
	- Supply shocks
		- Oil crises
	- People's expectations about inflation
- Phillips curve is valid for short run periods, but shifts over longer periods

### Keynesian Policy for Fighting Unemployment and Inflation

- Expansionary fiscal policy
	- Tax cuts
	- Direct increases in government spending

- Contractionary fiscal policy
	- Economy is operating above potential GDP
	- Unemployment is low
	- Inflationary prices increase
	- Tax increases or government cuts
